## Overview

Goal: Developing complete, application-driven sensing microsystems by unifying fundamental device physics, custom electronics, and system-level field validation.

Our group works at the intersection of MEMS/microsystems, microfabrication, and system integration. The unifying theme is closing the gap between a promising device-level result and a deployable, field-validated microsystem — treating the sensing element, fluidic or mechanical actuation, readout and power electronics, and packaging and thermal environment as a single co-designed system rather than separately optimized blocks. Across microscale gas chromatography, self-powered wireless sensing, and solar sensing and control, the aim is the same: full end-to-end operation under conditions that resemble real deployment, not just isolated benchtop characterization.

### Gas Sensing via Microscale Gas Chromatography (μGC)

Microscale gas chromatography promises compact, low-cost, low-power chemical analysis for applications that need rapid, in situ, continuous quantification of a small number of known species — industrial process control, environmental monitoring, healthcare, forensics, and food quality. The field's central bottleneck is integration: μGC components are conventionally fabricated separately and interconnected in a hybrid fashion, which inflates size and cost. Monolithic integration is the obvious answer, but pumps and valves have resisted it because they need flexible diaphragms and mechanical actuators that are structurally and thermally incompatible with preconcentrators, columns, and detectors. Our group's contribution is a co-design philosophy that selects architectures whose fabrication steps, thermal budgets, and material sets are mutually compatible. The result is the first μGC system with all fluidic components monolithically integrated on a single 15 × 15 mm² chip — three Knudsen pumps, a preconcentrator, a separation column, and a capacitive detector. Knudsen pumps use thermal transpiration in narrow channels to induce flow with no moving parts, and a unique arrangement of three unidirectional pumps generates counterbalancing pressure heads that eliminate valves entirely. Crucially, the pumping channels are formed in thick, unsuspended oxide and silicon layers using standard deposition and etching — trading some thermal isolation for dramatically improved yield, fabrication simplicity, and integration compatibility. The system quantified species in alkene, glycol ether, aromatic, and mercaptan mixtures to ±8.5%, with <2.7% RSD in peak height and <4.7% RSD in retention time, stable from 15–100% RH, and resolved analytes spanning Kovats indices from ≈650 to ≈1000. More broadly, this demonstrates that a complete, self-sufficient gas analyzer can be mass-produced as a single chip rather than assembled as a custom instrument — an important step toward low-cost, distributed chemical sensing.

### Self-Powered Sensing Nodes via Advanced Energy Harvesting

Powering distributed IoT nodes without batteries remains a fundamental obstacle to long-term, maintenance-free sensing. Triboelectric nanogenerators (TENGs) are attractive for harvesting ubiquitous low-frequency mechanical energy (<10 Hz) where resonant harvesters fail, but their high-voltage, low-current pulsed output is severely mismatched to low-voltage electronics — so TENGs have mostly been used as self-powered sensors rather than genuine power sources. Prior solutions relied on high-frequency sources, multiple TENGs, or hybrid harvesters, all of which limit miniaturization or add complexity. Our group's approach is system-level co-design: matching the harvester, power management, and low-power sensing and communication electronics from the start. Using a synchronous electric charge extraction (SECE) strategy with cold-start capability and a flyback topology with a high-voltage GaN switch, a single 10 × 10 cm TENG operating at 5 Hz powers a complete microsystem — harvester, power management, capacitive VOC sensor, and BLE MCU — delivering ~110 μW, a five-fold improvement over conventional full-bridge rectification, while total system consumption stays below 94.4 μW. The system cold-starts from 0 V and sustains continuous wireless operation, which to our knowledge is the first demonstration of its kind under low-frequency excitation. The broader impact is a pathway toward battery-less IoT nodes for wearable, environmental, and industrial monitoring, where system-level integration — not just harvester performance — is what makes continuous operation possible.

### Solar Sensing and Control Systems

Large-scale photovoltaic (PV) systems are a rapidly expanding contributor to sustainable energy, but as PV cell efficiency approaches physical limits, further gains increasingly depend on power management — solar tracking, maximum power point tracking (MPPT), and array reconfiguration — all of which rely on accurate, real-time measurements of solar angle and intensity. Conventional solar sensors are bulky, measure only one parameter, and offer a narrow field of view, making them poorly suited for distributed, in-situ deployment across large PV installations. Astronomical algorithms avoid hardware but lack real-time feedback and degrade under installation misalignment or transient environmental effects, while indirect intensity estimation from array output cannot resolve localized shading, soiling, or panel-to-panel differences. The core challenge is a fundamental tradeoff in miniaturized solar sensors: wide field of view, high angular accuracy, and manufacturability have historically come at each other's expense, and almost all prior single-chip devices measure angle alone. Our group addresses this with a multifunctional integrated solar sensor (MISS) that simultaneously measures solar angle and intensity on a single 9 × 9 mm² chip. Three interdigitated back-contact (IBC) detectors are fabricated with differently inclined front surfaces using anisotropic KOH etching, which broadens the field of view without mechanical movement; a physics-based fitting model accounts for nonidealities such as lateral carrier drift and optical path loss, and an intensity-independent parameter derived from the three detector outputs decouples angle from intensity. The device achieves an unprecedented ±75° field of view with a mean angular error of 3.4° and a mean relative intensity error of 1.6%, with the relative error decreasing further at the nominal solar intensity of 1000 W/m² relevant to PV operation. By providing compact, wide-FOV, in-situ measurement of both parameters on-chip, the MISS enables the distributed, real-time sensing that advanced PV power management systems require — and points toward broader use in space-based solar monitoring and distributed environmental sensing.
